01
The business problem
Why 1,618 exceptions can't all be reviewed the same way
L2 classified every one of the 99,441 orders into exactly one bucket — 97,823 matched cleanly, and 1,618 didn't. Those 1,618 span four very different underlying problems: money already collected on a canceled order, missing item records, a logging gap in the approval step, and expected installment-interest variance. Today, an analyst reviewing this queue has no way to tell, at a glance, which of the 1,618 is worth 10 minutes of attention and which is worth 30 seconds.
What "manual review" looks like without this workflow
Every exception gets opened, read, and reasoned about from scratch — regardless of whether it's a R$4,809 cash-recovery case or a R$0.00 approval-logging gap. Without a scoring or prioritization layer, the analyst's queue is effectively unordered, and documentation quality varies by whoever reviewed it and how much time they had that day.

Steps 1–3 are pure SQL business rules — the same l4_risk_assessment and l4_prioritized_queue tables verified against L2/L3's 1,618 total. Step 4 is the only step where AI touches the process, and it only drafts context. Step 5 is where a human actually decides. Step 6 records what happened, independent of the AI's draft.

Tier is not the same scale as L2's severity. L2 assigns an absolute severity per exception type across the whole population (e.g. CANCELED_WITH_PAYMENT is always HIGH, INSTALLMENT_INTEREST is always EXP/expected). L4's risk_tier is a relative ranking built for triage order, not a restatement of L2's severity: NTILE(4) is computed separately within each of the 6 exception types, not across the 4 business categories. This is deliberate — if quartiles were computed per business category instead, the 14 small-dollar APPROVAL_GAP orders and the near-zero-dollar UNDERPAYMENT/OVERPAYMENT orders would be swamped by CANCELED_WITH_PAYMENT's much larger amounts inside the same Cash Recovery Risk category, and would never reach CRITICAL. Ranking within exception type instead guarantees every type gets its own CRITICAL/HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW spread, so a rare, easy-to-miss pattern (like a 14-order approval gap) still surfaces for review.

All 14 APPROVAL_GAP orders carry R$0.00 financial exposure — they're a control gap, not a dollar variance — so ranking by exposure alone leaves a 14-way tie. The query breaks that tie by order_purchase_timestamp (oldest first), so a CRITICAL APPROVAL_GAP means "one of the 4 oldest unresolved control gaps," a real and defensible signal, rather than an arbitrary split among equal-value rows. An INSTALLMENT_INTEREST case tiered CRITICAL means "review it before other installment-interest cases first," not "treat it like a cash recovery emergency."
CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE l4_risk_assessment AS
SELECT
    c.order_id, c.exception_type, o.order_purchase_timestamp,
    CASE c.exception_type
        WHEN 'CANCELED_WITH_PAYMENT' THEN 'Cash Recovery Risk'
        WHEN 'UNDERPAYMENT'          THEN 'Cash Recovery Risk'
        WHEN 'OVERPAYMENT'           THEN 'Cash Recovery Risk'
        WHEN 'MISSING_ITEMS'         THEN 'Data Integrity Risk'
        WHEN 'APPROVAL_GAP'          THEN 'Process Control Gap'
        WHEN 'INSTALLMENT_INTEREST'  THEN 'Expected Variance'
    END AS business_category,
    ROUND(CASE WHEN c.exception_type = 'CANCELED_WITH_PAYMENT' THEN c.payment_total ELSE ABS(c.diff) END, 2) AS financial_exposure,
    NTILE(4) OVER (
        PARTITION BY c.exception_type
        ORDER BY (CASE WHEN c.exception_type = 'CANCELED_WITH_PAYMENT' THEN c.payment_total ELSE ABS(c.diff) END) DESC,
                 o.order_purchase_timestamp ASC  -- tie-break: oldest unresolved case ranks higher
    ) AS exposure_quartile
FROM l2_exception_classified c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON c.order_id = o.order_id
WHERE c.exception_type != 'OK';

CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE l4_prioritized_queue AS
SELECT *,
    CASE exposure_quartile WHEN 1 THEN 'CRITICAL' WHEN 2 THEN 'HIGH' WHEN 3 THEN 'MEDIUM' ELSE 'LOW' END AS risk_tier
FROM l4_risk_assessment
ORDER BY
    CASE business_category
        WHEN 'Cash Recovery Risk' THEN 1 WHEN 'Data Integrity Risk' THEN 2
        WHEN 'Process Control Gap' THEN 3 WHEN 'Expected Variance' THEN 4 END,
    exposure_quartile;

03
Why human-in-the-loop
AI should assist financial exception review, not make financial decisions
Financial decisions — approving a write-off, rejecting a refund, escalating a case — remain the responsibility of a human analyst. The AI step in this workflow is scoped narrowly on purpose:
What the AI does
Reads the fields already computed by SQL (category, tier, exposure, order status, payment/order totals) and drafts a plain-language summary, a possible cause, and a recommended action — grounded strictly in those fields, with an explicit instruction not to invent facts it wasn't given.
What the AI does not do
It does not classify the exception (SQL already did that in L2). It does not assign the risk tier (SQL already did that in Step 1–2). It does not approve, reject, or write anything back to the ledger. Every one of those stays a human action in Step 5.
This scoping is what makes the workflow defensible in an interview: every step before the AI is traceable back to a SQL query, and the AI's own output carries a visible disclosure that it requires human validation — shown live in the demo below.
